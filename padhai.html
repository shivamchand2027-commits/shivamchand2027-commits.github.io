1. How a venture fund makes money

A venture fund raises capital from limited partners (LPs): pension funds, endowments, family offices and high-net-worth individuals. A fund typically lives for about 10 years. The fund manager (general partner, GP) earns a management fee (commonly around 2% a year) and a share of profits called carry (commonly around 20%).

The core idea: a VC is not looking for a good company. It is looking for a fund-returner. Most investments fail or return little, and a few outliers produce nearly all of the return. This is the power law.

Illustrative maths. A $100M fund must return about 3x, or $300M, to LPs. Suppose the fund owns 10% of a company at entry and is diluted to about 7% by exit. To return $300M from one company, that exit must be roughly $4B. This is why investors ask whether a company can become a $1B+ business. If your honest answer is a strong, profitable business of a smaller size, venture capital may not be the right path. Bootstrapping, angels, debt and grants are valid alternatives.

2. How investors evaluate a company

First screening often takes just 2 to 5 minutes, so every point needs to be clear quickly. Most investors weigh seven things:

  1. Team. Why are these founders the right people for this problem? Do the co-founders have complementary skills and mutual trust? At early stage the team is the strongest signal.
  2. Market size. Build it bottom-up: number of customers x annual revenue per customer. Top-down claims such as "if we win 1% of a huge market" are discounted.
  3. Problem and product. How painful is the problem, and will customers pay to solve it? What is your unfair advantage: data, distribution, technology or supply?
  4. Traction. Revenue, growth rate, retention and customer quality. Early on, retention says more than growth: adding users to a leaky bucket does not build a business.
  5. Unit economics. Customer acquisition cost (CAC), lifetime value (LTV), payback period and gross margin. A common rule of thumb is LTV/CAC of 3 or more, though context varies by sector.
  6. Business model and moat. How does the company make money, and what protects it if a competitor copies it?
  7. The round. How much are you raising, what will it fund, and how many months of runway does it buy?
Rule of thumb: raise enough to reach the next milestone that makes the following round easier, with 18 to 24 months of runway. "Our competitor raised this much" is a weak reason.

What belongs in a pitch deck (10 to 12 slides)

  1. One-line description of what you do
  2. Problem
  3. Solution and demo
  4. Market size (bottom-up)
  5. Traction
  6. Business model
  7. Go-to-market
  8. Competition and positioning
  9. Team
  10. Financials and unit economics
  11. The ask: amount, use of funds and runway

Due diligence

After a term sheet, investors run diligence: cap table, incorporation and IP documents, customer references, financials and bank statements, tax filings and founder backgrounds. Keep the cap table clean, hold IP in the company's name, and make sure the numbers in your deck match your books. A small inconsistency can end a deal.

3. How valuation is set

Reality check: early-stage valuation is closer to negotiation than to science. Methods give a basis for the conversation, but the final number reflects demand, competition between investors and your leverage.

Key terms

  • Pre-money valuation: company value before the new investment.
  • Post-money valuation: pre-money plus the new investment.
  • Investor ownership: investment divided by post-money valuation.
  • Dilution: your ownership after the round = previous ownership x (1 minus the new investor's percentage).

Example. Pre-money of 20 and an investment of 5 gives a post-money of 25. The investor owns 5/25 = 20%, and founders go from 100% to 80% (an option pool can dilute further).

Common methods

MethodBest used forHow it works
VC methodSeed to Series A reasoningEstimate exit value in 5 to 7 years, the investor's target multiple (for example 10x) and dilution from later rounds. Post-money = exit value x (1 minus future dilution) / target multiple.
ComparablesWhen similar recent rounds are knownLook at revenue multiples or round sizes of companies at the same stage, sector and geography.
Scorecard / BerkusPre-revenue angel roundsAdjust a regional average valuation up or down for team, market, product and competition. Berkus assigns value to the idea, prototype, team, relationships and sales.
DCFLate stage, predictable cash flowDiscount forecast cash flows to today's value. It is of limited use early on because the forecast is itself an estimate.

For companies with revenue, an ARR multiple is also a common reference. Multiples move with the market cycle, so check current comparables instead of memorising a number.

Is a higher valuation always better? No. A very high valuation requires a larger milestone to justify the next round. If you miss it, the result can be a flat or down round and strained investor relationships. Choose terms that make the next round easier.

4. Funding readiness: when to raise

Fundraising is close to a full-time job. It can take 3 to 6 months and pulls attention from the business. Ask yourself first:

  • Will capital remove my main bottleneck? If the bottleneck is the product or customer demand rather than money, fix that first.
  • Can I state clearly what the money achieves in the next 18 months?
  • Is the cap table clean? Inactive co-founder equity, side promises and unregistered options all surface in diligence.
  • Can I explain the numbers on one page? Revenue, growth, retention, burn and runway.

Preparation checklist

  1. Incorporate properly and put founder shares on vesting (commonly 4 years with a 1-year cliff).
  2. Assign IP and domains to the company.
  3. Keep the cap table in a single spreadsheet.
  4. Build a simple monthly financial model for 18 to 24 months.
  5. Prepare a data room: deck, model, cap table, incorporation documents and key contracts.
  6. List 20 to 30 target investors and read each one's thesis. The Fund Vault is a good starting point.

India-specific note. Many early rounds use iSAFE notes, CCPS or convertible notes. Speak to a lawyer and a chartered accountant about structure. Foreign investment also raises company-structure, FEMA and tax questions.

5. Reading a term sheet

A term sheet is mostly non-binding (apart from clauses such as exclusivity and confidentiality), but it sets the direction of the deal. Read these carefully:

TermWhat it means
Valuation and round sizePre-money value, investment amount and the investor's resulting ownership.
Liquidation preferenceHow much the investor is paid first on an exit. 1x non-participating is generally considered founder-friendly. Participating or above 1x can significantly reduce your share.
Option poolShares reserved for employees. Investors often require the pool in the pre-money, which increases founders' effective dilution.
Anti-dilutionProtection for the investor in a down round. Broad-based weighted average is common. Full ratchet is heavy on founders.
Board and controlBoard seats and protective provisions: which decisions need investor approval.
VestingHow founder shares are earned and what happens if a founder leaves.
Pro-rata / ROFRThe investor's right to maintain its percentage in later rounds.
Exclusivity (no-shop)A period during which you cannot talk to other investors.
Advice: do not sign the first term sheet without a startup lawyer reviewing it. Terms can cost more than valuation.

6. Reaching investors

  1. Warm introductions beat cold email. Use portfolio founders, angels, mentors and alumni networks. When you ask for an introduction, send a short blurb the introducer can forward.
  2. Choose the right investors. Check that stage, sector and cheque size match. Use the Fund Vault filters, then read each fund's portfolio.
  3. Run a process. Book 15 to 20 meetings within a short window to build momentum.
  4. Follow up. Send a brief update after each meeting. If an investor says "let's talk later", return with a new milestone.

Cold email template

Subject: [Company] - [what you do in one line] | [strongest number] Hi [Name], I am [Name], founder of [Company]. We [what you do] for [who], and [one proof point: users, revenue or pilot]. Recent numbers: [metric 1], [metric 2], [growth over the last 3 months]. I am raising a [stage] round of [amount] to reach [milestone]. Your work with [portfolio company or thesis] looks closely related. Deck and a 2-minute demo: [link]. Could we speak for 20 minutes next week? Best regards, [Name] | [LinkedIn] | [phone]

7. Common founder mistakes

  • Mass emailing without research. Investors notice quickly, especially with the wrong name.
  • Top-down market sizing. "If we get 1% of the market" is not a plan.
  • "We have no competitors." There is always at least the status quo.
  • Insisting on a high valuation without traction.
  • A messy cap table: co-founders without vesting, verbal equity promises.
  • Raising with two months of runway left. It signals urgency and removes your leverage.
  • Overstating numbers. Diligence will surface them.
  • Looking only at the money. You will work with an investor for 7 to 10 years. Check reputation, support and references.
